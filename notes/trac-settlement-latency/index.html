Halyard Notes

How long a write takes to settle on a Trac subnet

r2rlabs · 26 September 2026

We needed a number, could not find one, so we measured it and are publishing the result and the harness. If you are building on Trac and have wondered how long your writes actually take to agree, this is one data point with the method attached so you can get your own.

What this is not: a benchmark of Trac. We measured one subnet, from one node, on one home connection, over twenty samples. It says nothing about the network's capacity, its throughput, or how fast it can go — only how long our writes took to come back agreed from where we were sitting. For anything about the network itself, read Trac's own documentation rather than our numbers.

Why we cared

We are building a perpetual futures exchange where every price is verified inside a Trac contract. That means a price has a shelf life: too old and it should not be traded on. Setting that threshold needs three numbers, and we had two of them.

StepMeasuredHow
Fetch a signed price from Pyth's Hermes518 ms100 fetches
Verify its signature inside the contract4.5 ms1,000 runs
Settle it on Trac?—

Settlement was the whole budget and we were guessing at it. So we stopped guessing.

What we ran

A subnet on Trac mainnet running our Pyth oracle contract, and a harness that submits real signed price updates and times two things per sample: how long the broadcast call takes to return, and how long until the price is readable from the signed view — which is the number that matters, because that is when the network has agreed rather than when our client stopped talking.

Each transaction costs a flat 0.03 TNK, so twenty samples cost 0.6 TNK. Nothing failed.

The result

Twenty samples, warm node:

Seconds
Median10.4
p9026.3
Max28.7

The first five samples, in order:

41s21s8s10s10s

That first 41 seconds is the most useful thing we learned. It is not consensus — it is a cold node finding peers and opening connections. Every later sample on the same node was under 30 seconds. If you are building something a person waits for, warm the connection up before they press anything, because otherwise your first user action looks broken and your later ones look fine.

The tail matters more than the median for us. A median of 10 seconds is livable. A p90 of 26 seconds means one write in ten takes nearly half a minute, and anything built on the assumption of ten will misbehave regularly.

What we changed because of it

We had planned a 30-second staleness threshold — a price older than that cannot be traded on. The measurement said that was wrong in the direction we did not expect: a perfectly normal 28-second settlement was one bad moment away from rejecting honest trades.

So we raised it to 60 seconds, and raised the gap threshold to 120. That sounds like weakening a safety rule, and it would be, if staleness were the only protection. It is not. Every order carries the worst price its owner will accept, checked at fill time, so the economic protection is the price band and staleness is just a blunt outer limit. Tightening the wrong dial would have cost users fills without making them safer.

Four things that cost us a day

These are the parts we would have wanted to read before starting.

A fresh install breaks hypercore writes. npm install pulled hyperschema 1.26.2, which wants compact-encoding v3, while hypercore-storage wants v2. The symptom is Cannot read properties of null (reading 'length') on any write, which looks like your bug and is not. Pin hyperschema to 1.19.0 — the version trac-peer's own lockfile uses.

A broadcast with no connected validators returns a bare false. Not an error, not a reason. If your node has not finished finding peers, you get nothing back and no clue why. Wait for connections before submitting anything.

Validators on the legacy protocol send no reply at all. The client waits for a response that will never arrive and times out after nine seconds by default, long before the transaction actually settles. We wait for the transaction to appear in the unsigned state instead, with the timeout raised to sixty seconds.

"Broadcast failed" sometimes means it worked. We deployed our subnet three times, each apparently failing, before finding result code 38 — BOOTSTRAP_ALREADY_EXISTS. The first attempt had succeeded. Ask the network what state it is in rather than trusting the client's success or failure message; we now have a check-subnet script that does exactly that, and it saved us from deploying a fourth.

Caveats, stated plainly

  • One subnet, one node, twenty samples. A larger sample would give a better tail.
  • Measured from a home connection in one location. Your latency to peers is not ours.
  • Our node was a writer on its own subnet. A busier subnet with more writers may behave differently.
  • Some validators were on the legacy protocol during our run. That mix will change over time, and so may these numbers.
  • We did not measure throughput, and nothing here should be read as a throughput figure.

The code

The harness is scripts/measure-settlement.mjs in our oracle proof of concept, which is public. It submits real Pyth updates, needs a funded MSB address, and prints every sample rather than only the summary, so you can see the shape rather than trust our arithmetic. If you run it and get different numbers, we would genuinely like to know — one data point is not a finding.
